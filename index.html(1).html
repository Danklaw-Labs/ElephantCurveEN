German economist Christoph Lakner and Serbian economist Branko Milanovic published the paper “Global Income Distribution: From the Fall of the Berlin Wall to the Great Recession” in 2015, a study that assembled one of the most comprehensive databases of household income surveys ever compiled for inequality research. The timeframe also incidentally sparked a major debate on the effects of globalism.

At the time of publication, the curve resembled an elephant, with a steeply rising back, a sharp descent toward near zero, and an upswing at the end. The methodology drew on national household surveys from over 100 countries and harmonized them to 2005 purchasing power parity (PPP) dollars. The period revealed two geopolitical ruptures: the fall of communism, which opened vast new labor markets, and the Global Financial Crisis, which abruptly ended the era of high globalization. Crucially, the data represent real per-capita income growth adjusted for inflation, not nominal changes, making cross-country comparisons meaningful.

(Bottom 50%)

The study concluded that some of the poorest regions; sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, were benefiting little from the liberal free-trade world order because their base earnings were low to begin with; many remained below $1.25/day even after two decades of growth.

(Middle 40%)

The most celebrated winners were China, India, Thailand, Vietnam, and Indonesia, where well over 500 million people were lifted out of the extreme poverty threshold of $1.25/day. However, as Milanovic himself noted, China does most of the statistical lifting here due to its massive rural-to-urban transition.

(Top 10%)

The massive dip, however, is found among the middle classes of high-income countries, whose incomes have stagnated or increased only minimally. The stagnation is often cited as a leading factor for the rise of populism throughout the highly developed world.

(Top 1%)

Yet in the same highly developed parts of the world; the United States, East Asia, and Western Europe, some of the strongest growth was seen among the richest people. Since their base income was already massive, any small percentage increase translated into massive amounts of additional income. More notably, 50% of the top 1% resided in the United States.

Follow-up studies have drawn four conclusions.

  1. First, globalism has barely improved the economies of the poorest countries on Earth.
  2. Second, “middle” countries, especially China, have benefited from globalism.
  3. Third, the middle-class populations of rich, developed countries experienced no real wage growth or only minimal growth in real terms.
  4. Fourth, the biggest winners of all are the already wealthy, who are better able to exploit and sustain opportunities around the world to expand their already huge wealth.
